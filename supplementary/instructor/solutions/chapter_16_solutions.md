# Solutions Manual - Chapter 16: Statement of Cash Flows

## Questions - Solutions

### Question 1: Purpose
The statement of cash flows explains how cash and cash equivalents changed during the period by classifying inflows/outflows into operating, investing, and financing activities. It complements the income statement and balance sheet by highlighting liquidity, solvency, and cash-generating ability.

### Question 2: Activity Categories
- **Operating:** Cash effects of core operations (customers, suppliers, payroll, taxes). PCN Class 4 accounts (e.g., 4011 customers, 44111 suppliers, 461411 VAT) drive adjustments.
- **Investing:** Purchases/disposals of long-term assets and investments (PCN Class 2). Includes capital expenditures, proceeds from asset sales.
- **Financing:** Owner and lender transactions (issuing shares, borrowing, repaying principal, dividends). PCN Class 1 accounts (e.g., 101 share capital, 1941/1942 bank loans) and 4712 dividends payable.

### Question 3: Indirect Method
Start with net income, adjust for non-cash items (depreciation, amortization, gains/losses) and working-capital changes (receivables, inventory, payables) to derive cash from operating activities. This method is standard under PCN/IFRS.

### Question 4: Information Needed
Income statement, comparative balance sheets, details on non-cash transactions (e.g., barter asset exchanges), supplemental data (cash paid for interest/taxes). For Luxembourg filings, maintain schedules supporting PCN account movements.

### Question 5: Luxembourg Requirements
Large entities (as defined by size criteria) must present a cash flow statement in annual accounts filed with RCS/eCDF. Format typically mirrors IAS 7: indirect method for operations, with notes detailing non-cash investing/financing transactions.

## Comprehensive Problem 16 - Solutions

### 1. Operating Activities (Indirect)
Net Income 120,000
Adjustments:
- Depreciation 25,000
- Amortization 3,000
- Loss on sale 2,000
Working-capital changes (using BS deltas):
- AR +10,000 → −10,000

- Prepaid +2,000 → −2,000
- AP +5,000 → +5,000

Total Operating Cash = 120,000 +25,000 +3,000 +2,000 −10,000 +5,000 −2,000 +5,000 +2,000 −2,000 = **€148,000**

### 2. Investing Activities
- Purchase equipment (cash) (50,000)
- Sale of equipment: cash inflow 8,000
**Net Investing = (€42,000)**

### 3. Financing Activities
- Loan repayment: (20,000)
- Dividends paid: (48,000)
(No new borrowings assumed)
**Net Financing = (€68,000)**

### 4. Statement Summary
Operating +148,000  
Investing −42,000  
Financing −68,000  
Net increase = **€38,000**

Net increase in cash €38,000 + beginning cash €30,000 = **ending cash €68,000**, which agrees with the balance sheet (cash €30,000 → €68,000).

Balance sheet check: 2023 assets 30,000 + 40,000 + 60,000 + 5,000 + 200,000 − 50,000 + 100,000 (intangibles) = €385,000 = liabilities + equity 25,000 + 8,000 + 12,000 + 100,000 + 150,000 + 90,000 = €385,000. 2024 assets 68,000 + 50,000 + 55,000 + 7,000 + 230,000 − 65,000 + 97,000 = €442,000 = 30,000 + 10,000 + 10,000 + 80,000 + 150,000 + 162,000 = €442,000. Account reconciliations: equipment 200,000 + 50,000 − 20,000 = 230,000; accumulated depreciation 50,000 + 25,000 − 10,000 = 65,000; intangibles 100,000 − 3,000 = 97,000; loan 100,000 − 20,000 = 80,000; retained earnings 90,000 + 120,000 − 48,000 = 162,000; loss on sale = book value 10,000 − proceeds 8,000 = 2,000.

### 5. Cash Flow Analysis
- **Operating cash flow:** Positive (€148k) indicating core activities generate liquidity.
- **Investing cash flow:** Negative (−€42k) due to equipment upgrades; monitor ROI on CAPEX.
- **Financing cash flow:** Negative (−€68k) as the entity services debt and pays dividends.
- **Implications:** Operations fund most outflows, but sustained dividend/repayment levels require continued operating strength or supplemental financing.

### 6. Luxembourg Compliance Notes
- Use the PCN account classifications for the underlying data; the statement itself is usually structured like IAS 7 (operating / investing / financing, indirect method). Small companies are generally not required to publish one, but larger companies may be required to file it with the RCS.
- Disclose non-cash investing/financing transactions (e.g., leases) in the notes.
- Interest and dividend cash payments must be shown separately in the notes (per IAS 7) even if classified as operating cash flows.
- Retain supporting schedules for audit/commissaire reporting.

## Case Solutions

### Case 16-1: Cash Flow vs. Profit
1. Profit includes accruals (depreciation, credit sales) that do not immediately affect cash.
2. Non-cash items: depreciation, amortization, accrual-based expenses/revenues, gains/losses.
3. Profit but negative cash flow occurs when working capital grows (e.g., receivables/inventory increases) or when large CAPEX/dividends consume cash.
4. Reconcile net income to operating cash via the indirect method to identify drivers.
5. Analyze trends in operating/investing/financing sections plus key ratios (cash conversion cycle).
6. Improve cash flow by tightening collections, managing inventory, negotiating supplier terms, and phasing CAPEX.

### Case 16-2: Cash Flow Management
1. The cash flow statement highlights whether issues stem from operations, investing, or financing.
2. Common causes: slow collections, excessive inventory, rapid growth without financing, heavy CAPEX, high debt service.
3. Improve operating cash by accelerating receivables, optimizing inventory, revisiting pricing, and controlling discretionary spend.
4. Persistent negative operating cash threatens solvency; investigate turnaround actions or external financing.
5. Build rolling cash forecasts, scenario plans, and covenant monitoring; align with Luxembourg tax/VAT payment schedules.
6. Luxembourg requirements: include cash flow statement for larger entities, file via eCDF/RCS, and document any deviations or exemptions.
