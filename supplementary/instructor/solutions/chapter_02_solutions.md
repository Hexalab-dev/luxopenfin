# Solutions Manual - Chapter 2: Introduction to Financial Statements

## Questions - Solutions

### Question 1: Income Statement vs. Balance Sheet

**Answer:**

**Income Statement (Compte de Résultat):**
- **What it shows:** Revenues, expenses, and net income (or net loss) for a business
- **Time period:** Covers a period of time (e.g., "For the month ended November 30, 2024" or "For the year ended December 31, 2024")
- **Purpose:** Shows whether the business is profitable during the period
- **Key equation:** Revenue - Expenses = Net Income (or Net Loss)
- **Example:** Shows sales of €20,000, expenses of €15,000, resulting in net income of €5,000

**Balance Sheet (Bilan):**
- **What it shows:** Assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time
- **Time period:** Shows position at a specific date (e.g., "As of December 31, 2024")
- **Purpose:** Shows what the business owns, owes, and the owner's interest
- **Key equation:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity
- **Example:** Shows cash of €10,000, equipment of €25,000, liabilities of €15,000, and equity of €20,000

**Key Difference:** Income Statement shows performance over time, while Balance Sheet shows position at a point in time.

### Question 2: Financial Statement Interconnections

**Answer:**

The four financial statements are interconnected as follows:

1. **Income Statement → Statement of Owner's Equity**
   - Net Income (or Net Loss) from the Income Statement flows to the Statement of Owner's Equity
   - Net Income increases equity; Net Loss decreases equity

2. **Statement of Owner's Equity → Balance Sheet**
   - Ending Equity from the Statement of Owner's Equity appears on the Balance Sheet
   - The equity section of the Balance Sheet shows the final equity amount

3. **Balance Sheet → Statement of Cash Flows**
   - Beginning and ending cash balances from the Balance Sheet are used in the Statement of Cash Flows
   - Changes in assets and liabilities affect cash flow calculations

4. **Income Statement → Statement of Cash Flows**
   - Net Income is the starting point for the operating activities section
   - However, adjustments are needed because net income includes non-cash items

**Example Flow:**
- Income Statement shows Net Income of €5,000
- Statement of Owner's Equity: Beginning Equity €20,000 + Net Income €5,000 = Ending Equity €25,000
- Balance Sheet shows Equity of €25,000 (matches Statement of Owner's Equity)
- Statement of Cash Flows explains why cash changed (may differ from net income due to non-cash items)

### Question 3: Current vs. Noncurrent Assets

**Answer:**

**Current Assets:** Assets expected to be converted to cash or used within one year

**Examples:**
1. **Cash** - Already in cash form, used for daily operations
2. **Accounts Receivable** - Expected to be collected within one year
3. **Inventory** - Expected to be sold within one year

**Noncurrent Assets:** Long-term assets not expected to be converted to cash within one year

**Examples:**
1. **Equipment** - Used over multiple years, not for immediate sale
2. **Buildings** - Long-term asset, used for business operations over many years
3. **Vehicles** - Used over multiple years for business operations

**Classification Reason:** Current assets represent short-term resources for operations, while noncurrent assets represent long-term investments in the business.

### Question 4: Accounting Equation and Balance Sheet

**Answer:**

**Accounting Equation:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity

**Relationship to Balance Sheet:**
- The Balance Sheet is directly based on the accounting equation
- **Left side (Assets):** Shows what the business owns
- **Right side (Liabilities + Equity):** Shows what the business owes and the owner's interest
- The equation must always balance: Total Assets = Total Liabilities + Total Equity

**Example:**
- Assets: €100,000
- Liabilities: €40,000
- Equity: €60,000
- Equation: €100,000 = €40,000 + €60,000 ✓ (balances)

**Why it matters:** If the Balance Sheet doesn't balance, there's an error in recording transactions.

### Question 5: Revenue vs. Expenses

**Answer:**

**Revenue:**
- Increases in equity from business operations
- Money earned from selling goods or services
- Examples: Sales Revenue, Service Revenue

**Expenses:**
- Decreases in equity from business operations
- Costs incurred to generate revenue
- Examples: Salaries Expense, Rent Expense, Utilities Expense

**Relationship to Net Income:**
- **Net Income = Revenue - Expenses**
- If Revenue > Expenses: Net Income (profit)
- If Expenses > Revenue: Net Loss

**Example:**
- Revenue: €50,000
- Expenses: €35,000
- Net Income: €15,000

### Question 8: PCN Requirements

**Answer:**

**Why PCN is Required:**
1. **Standardization:** Ensures all businesses use the same account structure
2. **Comparability:** Allows stakeholders to compare financial statements across businesses
3. **Regulatory Compliance:** Legal requirement under Luxembourg Commercial Code
4. **Transparency:** Makes financial information understandable and verifiable
5. **International Recognition:** Aligns with international accounting standards

**Consequences of Not Following PCN:**
- **RCS Rejection:** Annual accounts may be rejected if not in PCN format
- **Fines:** Penalties for non-compliance (€250-€25,000, depending on the violation)
- **Legal Issues:** Violation of Commercial Code requirements
- **Business Disruption:** Cannot complete required filings
- **Stakeholder Confusion:** Financial statements not comparable or understandable
- **Audit Problems:** Auditors cannot verify compliance

**Compliance Requirement:** All Luxembourg businesses must use PCN classifications when preparing financial statements.

### Question 9: Net Income vs. Cash Flow

**Answer:**

A business can have positive net income but negative cash flow because:

**Net Income includes non-cash items:**
- Depreciation (expense but no cash outflow)
- Accrued revenues (revenue recognized but cash not yet received)
- Accrued expenses (expense recognized but cash not yet paid)

**Cash Flow is affected by:**
- Cash collections from customers (may differ from revenue timing)
- Cash payments to suppliers (may differ from expense timing)
- Purchases of assets (cash outflow but not an expense)
- Loan repayments (cash outflow but not an expense)
- Owner withdrawals (cash outflow but not an expense)

**Example:**
- Income Statement shows Net Income of €5,000
- But business:
  - Purchased €10,000 equipment (cash outflow, not expense)
  - Has €8,000 in accounts receivable (revenue but no cash yet)
  - Result: Negative cash flow despite positive net income

**Which Statements Show This:**
- **Income Statement:** Shows Net Income of €5,000
- **Statement of Cash Flows:** Shows negative cash flow, explains the difference
- **Balance Sheet:** Shows cash balance decreased despite net income

### Question 10: Equipment Purchase Transaction

**Answer:**

**Transaction:** Purchase of €10,000 equipment

**Balance Sheet Effect:**
- **Assets increase:** Equipment increases by €10,000
- **Assets decrease or Liabilities increase:** 
  - If paid in cash: Cash decreases by €10,000
  - If purchased on credit: Accounts Payable increases by €10,000

**PCN Class Accounts Involved:**
- **Class 2 (Fixed Assets):** Equipment (222) - increases
- **Class 5 (Financial Accounts):** Cash (5131) - decreases (if cash purchase)
- **OR Class 4 (Third-Party Accounts):** Accounts Payable (44111) - increases (if credit purchase)

**Example (Cash Purchase):**
- Before: Assets €50,000, Liabilities €20,000, Equity €30,000
- Transaction: Equipment +€10,000, Cash -€10,000
- After: Assets €50,000 (Equipment +€10,000, Cash -€10,000), Liabilities €20,000, Equity €30,000
- Equation still balances: €50,000 = €20,000 + €30,000

### Problem B-2: Entity Form Comparison

**Solution:**

**a) SARL (Société à Responsabilité Limitée):**
```
EQUITY
  Share Capital                              €20,000
  Retained Earnings                          15,000
                                            ───────
Total Equity                                €35,000
```

**b) SNC Partnership (Société en Nom Collectif):**
```
EQUITY
  Partner A, Capital                         €15,000
  Partner B, Capital                         15,000
  Current Year Profit (split equally):
    Partner A Share                           5,000
    Partner B Share                           5,000
                                            ───────

```

**c) Individual Business:**
```
EQUITY
  Owner's Capital                            €35,000
                                            ───────
Total Equity                                €35,000
```

**Differences and Why:**
- **SARL:** Shows Share Capital (legal requirement) and Retained Earnings (accumulated profits). Equity is separate from owners' personal assets.

- **Individual Business:** Simple structure with Owner's Capital. No legal separation between business and owner.

**Why They Exist:**
- Different legal forms have different requirements for equity presentation
- SARL provides limited liability protection
- SNC allows partnership structure with profit sharing
- Individual business is simplest form, no legal entity separation

### Problem B-4: Luxembourg Compliance

**Solution:**

**a) Statements Required:**
- Income Statement (Compte de Résultat)
- Balance Sheet (Bilan)
- Notes to Financial Statements (Annexe)
- Statement of Cash Flows (if required based on size)

**b) PCN Requirements:**
- Must use PCN account classifications (Classes 1-7)
- Accounts must be properly coded with PCN numbers
- Financial statements must follow PCN format
- All accounts must be classified according to PCN standards

**c) Filing Deadline:**
- Must be filed within 7 months of fiscal year end
- Example: If fiscal year ends December 31, must file by July 31

**d) Filing Location:**
- Filed with RCS (Registre de Commerce et des Sociétés)
- Filed electronically through RCS portal
- Must be signed by company representatives

**e) Consequences of Non-Compliance:**
- **Late Filing Penalties:** €250-€25,000 depending on the violation
- **RCS Rejection:** Accounts may be rejected if not in proper format
- **Legal Consequences:** Potential criminal charges for serious violations
- **Business Disruption:** Cannot complete required business operations
- **Reputation Damage:** Public records show compliance issues
- **Tax Issues:** May affect tax filings and cause additional penalties

#### 6. SARL Filing Requirements

**Filing Requirements:**
- Must file annual accounts with RCS within 7 months of fiscal year end
- Must be filed electronically through RCS portal
- Must include Income Statement, Balance Sheet, and Notes
- Must be signed by company managers

**Format Requirements:**
- Must follow PCN format and classifications
- Must use PCN account numbers
- Must be in French, German, or English
- Must include required disclosures based on company size

**Size Considerations:**
- Small companies (not exceeding 2 of 3 limits for two consecutive years: balance sheet total ≤ €7.5M, net turnover ≤ €15M, average employees ≤ 50) may file abbreviated accounts
- Larger companies must file full accounts with more detailed disclosures

#### 7. Impact of Higher Sales (€3.5M)

**a) Financial Statement Type:**
- With €3.5M sales, the company is still **below** the small company thresholds (net turnover ≤ €15M, balance sheet total ≤ €7.5M, average employees ≤ 50; 2 of 3 for two consecutive years)
- It may still prepare **abbreviated financial statements** (comptes abrégés)
- Full statements (comptes complets) would only be required if it exceeded 2 of the 3 limits for two consecutive years

**b) Audit Requirements:**
- No statutory audit required: the audit obligation applies only when 2 of the 3 limits (balance sheet total > €7.5M, net turnover > €15M, average employees > 50) are exceeded for two consecutive years
- A voluntary audit is still possible (e.g., if the bank requests it)
- If an audit becomes mandatory later, it is performed by an approved statutory auditor (réviseur d'entreprises agréé)

**c) Disclosure Requirements:**
- Abbreviated notes remain sufficient as a small company
- Growth in sales should still be monitored against the thresholds each year
- Related party transactions, contingencies and commitments must still be disclosed where required
- Better internal reporting will be needed for management even if legal disclosures do not change

### Case 2-1: Understanding Financial Statements

**Solution:**

#### 1. Net Income vs. Cash Change

**Explanation to Marie:**

Net Income (€2,500) doesn't equal cash change (€1,000) because:

- **Net Income includes non-cash items:**
  - Depreciation (expense but no cash paid)
  - Accrued revenues (revenue recognized but cash not yet received)
  - Accrued expenses (expense recognized but cash not yet paid)

- **Cash is affected by other transactions:**
  - Purchases of equipment (cash outflow but not expense)
  - Loan repayments (cash outflow but not expense)
  - Owner withdrawals (cash outflow but not expense)
  - Collections from customers (may differ from revenue timing)
  - Payments to suppliers (may differ from expense timing)

**Statement to Help:**
- **Statement of Cash Flows** explains why cash changed
- Shows cash from operating, investing, and financing activities
- Reconciles Net Income to cash change

**Example:**
- Net Income: €2,500
- But: Purchased equipment €1,000 (cash outflow)
- Result: Cash increased by €1,500 (€2,500 - €1,000), not €2,500

#### 2. Equipment Value Difference

**Explanation to Marie:**

The Equipment on the Balance Sheet (€15,000) is different from what she paid (€18,000) because of **depreciation**.

**Accounting Concept: Depreciation**
- Equipment is recorded at cost (€18,000) when purchased
- Over time, depreciation expense reduces the book value
- Balance Sheet shows **net book value** (cost minus accumulated depreciation)
- Example: €18,000 cost - €3,000 accumulated depreciation = €15,000 net book value

**Why This Happens:**
- Assets lose value over time through use
- Depreciation allocates the cost over the asset's useful life
- Balance Sheet shows current value, not original cost

**This is correct accounting** - not an error by the accountant.

#### 3. VAT Payable Explanation

**VAT Payable (€1,500) is a Liability:**

- **What it is:** Money the business owes to the tax authority (AED) for VAT collected
- **Why it appears:** When Marie sells goods/services, she collects VAT from customers
- **Obligation:** She must remit this VAT to the tax authorities
- **Balance Sheet location:** Under Current Liabilities (Class 4)

**Example:**
- Marie sells €1,000 of restaurant meals (excluding VAT)
- Adds 3% VAT (meals and non-alcoholic drinks served on premises): €30
- Customer pays: €1,030
- Marie owes €30 to the AED = VAT Payable (alcoholic drinks she serves would carry 17%)

**This is money Marie owes, not money owed to her.**

### Case 2-2: Choosing a Legal Entity Form

**Solution:**

**Key Considerations (Questions 1–4):**

1. **Liability Protection:**
   - SARL: Limited liability (personal assets protected)
   - SNC: Unlimited liability (personal assets at risk)

2. **Equity Structure:**
   - SARL: Share Capital, easier to transfer ownership
   - SNC: Partner Capital accounts, more flexible profit sharing

3. **Tax Implications:**
   - SARL: Corporate tax, then personal tax on distributions
   - SNC: Pass-through taxation (partners taxed directly)

4. **Operational Requirements:**
   - SARL: More formal requirements, minimum capital €12,000
   - SNC: Less formal, no minimum capital requirement

5. **Filing Requirements:**
   - SARL: Annual accounts (balance sheet, P&L, notes) filed with the RCS within 7 months of year end; PCN chart balances filed on eCDF; corporate tax return (modèle 500) by 31 December of the following year
   - SNC: Books kept under the PCN; with expected turnover of €150,000 (above €100,000) the PCN balances must also be filed on eCDF; profits are taxed in the partners' personal returns

**Recommendation (Question 5):** For a consulting business with three partners who want limited liability, a SARL is typically better, especially for professional services where client claims are possible. The SNC is simpler and cheaper but exposes each partner's personal assets.

**Question 6 – Share capital:** The minimum share capital of a SARL is **€12,000**. The €30,000 can be contributed entirely as share capital, €10,000 per partner (e.g., 300 shares of €100 → 100 shares each), giving equal ownership consistent with the equal profit split:

